Evaluation · a layered lesson

Answer checkers

Our tutor answers from course passages. Before the student sees an answer, a small model reads each sentence and asks: do the cited passages really say this? This page explains how such a checker works, how good the known checkers are, and what that means for answers from a knowledge graph.

TL;DR

  • NLI (natural language inference) asks whether a premise text supports a hypothesis sentence. An answer checker does the same with two labels: supported or not.
  • MiniCheck and HHEM are small checkers of this kind. Our tutor uses MiniCheck to decide and logs HHEM beside it.
  • The tutor checks each answer sentence against the passages that the sentence cites, joined into one input of at most 2,048 tokens.
  • On the LLM-AggreFact leaderboard our MiniCheck scores 75.0 balanced accuracy, HHEM 71.8, the best open checker 77.4. On hard, recent cases every checker falls to about 50 (chance).
  • A baseline is the reference you compare against. For the graph arm of the thesis, the text arm is the strong baseline.
  • Checkers work on GraphRAG answers because the graph arm also returns passages. They miss facts that exist only as graph edges, so a flag means "needs review", not "wrong".
Level 0in one minute

A friend checks your quote

For anyone. No technical words.

You write a school report. Next to each sentence you put the page you took it from. A friend opens the page and reads it. For each sentence, your friend says one of three things.

"Yes, it says that."

Page: "I visited Iowa."

You wrote: "I visited the United States."

Iowa is in the United States, so the page supports you.

"It does not say that."

Page: "I visited the United States."

You wrote: "I visited Iowa."

Maybe true, but the page does not say which state.

"It says the opposite."

Page: "The capital of France is Berlin."

You wrote: "The capital of France is Paris."

You are right about the world, but not about the page. Your friend checks the page, not the world.

The three examples come from the HHEM model card .

An answer checker is that friend, made into a small computer program. It is fast and cheap, but it can be wrong. It also cannot tell you why.
Level 1for a student

Premise, hypothesis, score

For a student who knows what a language model is. One real example, worked.

The words

  • Premise: the text that we trust, here the course passages that the tutor retrieved.
  • Hypothesis: the sentence to check, here one sentence of the tutor's answer.
  • NLI labels: entailment (the premise supports it), neutral (the premise does not say), contradiction (the premise says the opposite).
  • Checker: a model that joins neutral and contradiction into one label, unsupported. It gives a score from 0 to 1. A cut-off (default 0.5) turns the score into a label .

How our tutor checks an answer

  1. Split the answer into sentences.
  2. For each sentence, find the passages it cites with markers such as [2]. A sentence without a marker gets all cited passages.
  3. Join those passages into one premise. If the premise has more than 2,048 tokens, mark the sentence "unchecked".
  4. Turn formulas into words. A sentence that is only a formula is "not checkable".
  5. Give MiniCheck the pair (premise, sentence). Read the score against the cut-off .

What a baseline is

A baseline is the thing you compare against. It tells the reader what "good" means. "Our checker is 75% accurate" says little. "Random guessing gives 50%, the old method 70%, ours 75%" says a lot (an illustrative example from the research note).

  • Trivial baseline: guess at random, or always say "supported". Balanced accuracy gives 50 for both.
  • Standard baseline: the method most people use today, for example AlignScore, or GPT-4 as a judge.
  • Strong baseline: the best known method, so that a win means something.
  • Ablation baseline: your own system with one part removed, to show what that part adds.

A fair baseline gets the same data, the same budget and the same tuning .
