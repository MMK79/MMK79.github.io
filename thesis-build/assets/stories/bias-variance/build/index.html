Bias and variance: why the most flexible model loses

Fit a curve to noisy points. Make it more flexible and it matches the training points better and better. Past some point it gets worse on new points. Move the slider and watch both happen with real numbers.

Everything below is computed in your browser. The data is synthetic: y = sin(2πx) + noise, with noise drawn from a Gaussian by a seeded random generator (seed 2026 for training and test data, seed 99 for the resampling in the last figure). Each fit is a least-squares polynomial solved by Householder QR in a Chebyshev basis.

2. Training error keeps falling. Test error does not.

Training error is measured on the points the curve was fitted to. Test error is measured on 400 other points from the same source. The gap between the two lines is what a flexible model buys by memorising noise. You can also click or tap a degree on the chart.

training errortest errornoise floor σ² (no model can go below it on average)

The vertical axis is logarithmic and stops at 1e-6; a training error below that (a curve through every point) is drawn on the bottom line, its exact value is in the readout above. A single dataset is noisy, so the lowest test point can move by a degree or two when you change n or σ. That is itself a symptom of variance.

3. Splitting the error: bias² + variance + noise

To see why test error is U-shaped, repeat the experiment many times. Draw 200 fresh training sets of size n, fit every degree to each, and look at the 200 predictions at each of 100 points along x.

Bias²: how far the average prediction is from the true curve. A curve that is too stiff is wrong on average. Variance: how much predictions move from one training set to the next. A curve that is too flexible chases the noise of whichever set it got. Noise: σ², the part nobody can predict.

Read it in plain words

Left of the sweet spot (underfitting). The curve is too simple. It misses the shape of the data, on training and test points alike. Bias² is large, variance is small. More flexibility helps.

Right of the sweet spot (overfitting). The curve is flexible enough to follow the noise. Training error is tiny, test error rises, and variance dominates. More data helps (raise n), and so does less flexibility.

Try this. Set the degree to 15 and n to 16: the curve passes through every training point and swings wildly in between. Raise n to 80: the same degree calms down. Raise σ: the noise floor rises and the best degree moves left.

What is computed and what is not

Computed in this page: the dataset (seeded), every polynomial fit, every error number, bias² and variance (by simulation, not by formula), the argmin degree and the region text.

Estimated, not exact: bias² and variance are Monte Carlo estimates from 200 resampled training sets. The simulated test error equals bias² + variance + σ² only up to simulation noise; the table prints the gap.

Illustrative choices: the true function (a sine), Gaussian noise, uniform x on [0,1], and the region rule (test error within 10% of the lowest counts as the sweet spot).
