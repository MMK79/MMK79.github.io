Chunking strategies

Most retrieval systems cut long documents into smaller pieces before they can search them. This page explains the common ways to cut, four times over: as a picture, for a student, with the research, and as the decision in our thesis. One real example runs through all of it: six minutes of a lecture on support vector machines.

TL;DR

  • A chunk is the unit that the retriever scores and, in a basic retriever, also the text that the language model reads. Where you cut decides whether one chunk holds a whole answer.
  • Seven common approaches: count characters, words or tokens (fixed), try big separators first (recursive), keep whole sentences, cut where the embedding distance between neighbouring sentences jumps (semantic), follow sections or pages (ours: pages and 5-minute video windows), search small children but return the big parent, or let an LLM rewrite the text into one-fact propositions. The first five choose cut positions; parent-child changes what is returned; propositions rewrite the text.
  • The evidence does not crown one rule. In the cited studies, embedding-breakpoint semantic chunking costs more and does not win consistently; the best size depends on the questions, the data and the embedder.
  • On our example the same question gets different evidence from each rule (see Touch).
  • Our thesis unit: one table-of-contents section of at most 1,024 bge-m3 tokens (longer sections are cut into children at paragraph, then sentence boundaries; lectures in sentence-aligned 5-minute windows), 8,515 indexable units over nine sources. The 1,600-character rule compared on this page is our earlier pilot batch rule for the lecture captions.
Level 0in one minute

A long paper strip and a pair of scissors

For anyone. No technical words.

Imagine the whole 75-minute lecture printed on one long paper strip. A friend asks: "Why is a bigger margin better?" You may hand over only three pieces of the strip. Before the question comes, you must cut the strip into pieces. How do you cut?

Every 20 cm

Fast and simple. But a cut can fall in the middle of a word, and the answer can end up half on one piece, half on the next.

At the end of each sentence or topic

The pieces usually read better on their own. But some topics are long, and a piece can become too big to hand over.

Small cards inside folders

Find the right small card, then hand over the whole folder it sits in. You find precisely and still give the context around it.

The same strip, cut three ways. The amber marks are the answer to the friend's question. Count how many pieces you need to cover them.

Takeaway: pieces too small lose the context; pieces too big drown the answer in other text. Most chunking strategies are rules for where the scissors go; others change what you hand over (the folder) or rewrite the pieces.
Level 1for a student

Seven rules, one lecture

For a student who knows what an embedding is: a vector that stands for the meaning of a text.

A retrieval system (RAG) stores each chunk with its embedding. At question time it embeds the question, finds the chunks with the closest vectors, and gives them to the language model. So the chunk is two things at once: the unit of matching (one vector per chunk) and the unit of context (the text the model reads). A chunk with two topics can get a vector that matches neither topic well; a chunk with half an idea gives the model half an answer. (Parent-child retrieval, below, separates the two roles.)

Below, each rule is shown on the same text: Lecture 14 of Caltech's Learning from Data, the minutes where Professor Abu-Mostafa argues that a fat margin is better. The examples in the right column are computed from the real captions.

Three pairs that people mix up

pair 1where you cut vs. how you embed

Chunking chooses the cut positions. Late chunking and contextual retrieval keep a given set of cuts but change what is indexed: late chunking encodes the whole document (or long windows of it) first and then pools the token vectors inside each chunk; contextual retrieval prepends a short LLM-written note that places this chunk in its document, before both embedding and keyword indexing.

pair 2what you match vs. what you return

In parent-child retrieval the vector of a 400-character child finds the place; the model then reads the 1,600-character parent. The unit you match and the amount of text you return are set separately.

pair 3overlap vs. bigger chunks

Overlap copies the last characters of one chunk into the next, so text near a cut also appears with its other side. A sentence longer than the overlap can still be split, and in our rule overlap never crosses a section. It stores more text (the table shows how much) but does not make the chunks themselves longer.

Ask a question

Retrieval searched all of Lecture 14 (about 70,000 characters), cut with each strategy's default settings, with bge-m3 dense cosine only (no keyword search, no reranker). "Marked answer characters covered" = the share of the characters of our answer quotes that are inside the three returned chunks (a recall of marked text, not a judgement of the answer). Propositions are rewritten text, so for them we count how many of the three come from a paragraph that holds a marked answer.
