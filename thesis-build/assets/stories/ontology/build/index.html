Ontology, and what a knowledge graph does with it

Start here: taxonomy, ontology and knowledge graph explained four times, from a shop to the thesis design (Levels 0-3), with one SVM example you can switch between the three layers. Then the deep dive: one small knowledge base about real machine-learning courses runs through the whole page. You edit triples, run a reasoner, validate extractions and retrieve with it. Every number on this page is computed in your browser, and the reasoner is checked against Python (rdflib, owlrl, pyshacl).

Data: university ML-course crawl (universities/exports/site/courses.json and the cached syllabus pages). three.js is not used: the ideas are graphs and rules, and 2D SVG shows them well.

Level 0in one minute

A shop: shelf signs, a rule book, and receipts

For anyone. No technical words.

Three words come up all the time: taxonomy, ontology and knowledge graph. Think of one small shop.

One shop, three kinds of description. The picture is ours.

Taxonomy = the shelf signs

Food, then Fruit, then Apples. In our shop every product has one place. You find a thing by walking down the signs.

Ontology = the rule book

It says which kinds of things exist and how they may be linked: a customer buys a product; dairy is a kind of product; dairy must be kept cold. In our shop it lists no real milk (a formal ontology may also hold some individuals).

Knowledge graph = the receipts

The real things of today, linked by those rules: Sara bought this milk, and it is in fridge 2. Each link has a receipt as supporting evidence.

In one line: a taxonomy sorts things. An ontology says what kinds of things exist, how they may be linked and what follows. A knowledge graph holds the real things and links, written in the words of the ontology.

Level 1for a student

The ladder, on one example: SVM

For a CS student. One running example from our ML course.

Each rung keeps what the rung below has and adds one thing. The ladder is a teaching device (Garshol 2004), not a standard. In practice the borders are not sharp.

  1. Term list (controlled vocabulary). One preferred label per concept (a controlled vocabulary can also manage synonyms). SVM · Perceptron · Kernel function · Dot product · Linear separability Adds: one meaning per term. Z39.19: All terms in a controlled vocabulary must have an unambiguous, non-redundant definition, as a design goal that does not always hold in practice (ANSI/NISO Z39.19, Sec. 4, p.5).
  2. Taxonomy. The terms in a hierarchy with one link type: broader and narrower. Z39.19 allows several parents (a "polyhierarchy", Sec. 5.4.3, p.18); our example is a plain tree. Machine learning > Supervised learning > Classification > SVM Adds: a place for each term. As you go down, you inherit from the top (Hart, video 01:14).
  3. Thesaurus. The tree plus synonyms and "related" links. SVM, also called "support vector network" (passage p109) · SVM related-to Kernel function Adds: other names and labelled relationships between terms (Z39.19 Sec. 5.4.4). SKOS is the W3C format for this rung and for taxonomies (SKOS Ref. Sec. 1.2).
  4. Ontology. Classes, named relations with a domain and a range, and axioms (rules). Classifier subClassOf Supervised method · uses: Method → Concept · prerequisite-of is transitive and has no cycle · a Method that uses a Kernel function is a Kernel method Adds: meaning a program can use. In OWL 2, objects are individuals, categories are classes, relations are properties (OWL 2 Primer, Sec. 3). A reasoner can now derive facts that nobody wrote down.
  5. Knowledge graph. Real nodes and edges in the words of the ontology, each edge with evidence. Passage p109 defines SVM · Lecture 14 covers SVM · Iris "setosa vs rest" example-of Linear separability Adds: data about the real world (Hogan et al. 2021, Sec. 1). "The ontology defines the grammar; the graph provides the evidence" (Packkildurai 2026). In our design every edge carries a supporting quote, so a tutor can show why. A quote supports an edge; it does not prove it: a curator still judges the reading.

Level 2professional and academic

Definitions, boxes and two kinds of world

For an engineer or a professor. Each claim has a source; the sources box at the end gives the page or section and whether we read it.

Ontology

Gruber: an ontology is an explicit specification of a conceptualization, and a conceptualization is an abstract, simplified view of the world that we wish to represent for some purpose (Gruber 1993, Sec. 1). Studer, Benjamins and Fensel merge this with Borst into a formal, explicit specification of a shared conceptualisation (Studer et al. 1998, p.25): formal = machine readable; explicit = the types of concepts and the constraints on their use are defined; shared = the knowledge is accepted by a group, not private. Guarino sees an ontology as a set of logical axioms designed to account for the intended meaning of a vocabulary; it can only approximate a set of intended models (Guarino 1998, Sec. 2.2).

Noy and McGuinness give the working view: concepts (classes), their properties (slots) and restrictions on slots (facets). An ontology together with a set of individual instances of classes constitutes a knowledge base. In reality, there is a fine line where the ontology ends and the knowledge base begins. (Noy & McGuinness 2001, Sec. 2, p.3)

Knowledge graph

Hogan et al. define a knowledge graph as a graph of data intended to accumulate and convey knowledge of the real world, whose nodes represent entities of interest and whose edges represent relations between these entities, and they say the definition "remains contentious" (Hogan et al. 2021, Sec. 1, p.2). Graphs give the option to forgo or postpone the definition of a schema (Sec. 3.1, p.12): a schema or ontology is optional. Ehrlinger and Wöß propose a stricter one: a knowledge graph acquires and integrates information into an ontology and applies a reasoner to derive new knowledge (Ehrlinger & Wöß 2016). So "a KG is the ontology applied to data" is one common usage, not the definition.

T-box, R-box, A-box

In description logic it is customary to split a knowledge base into three sets of axioms (Krötzsch, Simančík & Horrocks 2012, Sec. 1):

BoxHoldsIn our SVM domain
T-box (terminology)class axiomsClassifier ⊑ SupervisedMethod, ∃uses.KernelFunction ⊑ KernelMethod (a sufficient condition), Concept and Learning resource are disjoint (our design choice)
R-box (roles)property axiomsprerequisiteOf is transitive. (Domain and range can be written as T-box inclusions, ∃R.⊤ ⊑ C; on this page we show them with the relations, as a display convention.)
A-box (assertions)facts about individualssvm : Classifier, (p109, svm) : defines

The split is a convention: the logic treats all axioms alike, and OWL 2 does not use the words T-box or A-box (see topic tbox-abox-rbox). Your note also lists an X-Box (taxonomies) and a C-Box (contextual metadata). Both come from one blog post: Kurt Cagle, A-Box, T-Box, R-Box, C-Box, 2026-03-05. He calls the X-Box "tentative" and the box words "more of a memetic device" (Cagle 2026). We found no peer-reviewed use of either in the sources we checked (this is not proof that none exists). In some papers "CBox" means something else: a T-box plus an R-box (arXiv 2004.00291). In standard terms a taxonomy is a set of subclass axioms, so it is part of the T-box.

Open world and closed world

OWL uses the open-world assumption: information that is missing may be missing (but possibly true), and OWL 2 is not a schema language for syntax conformance (OWL 2 Primer, Sec. 2). By their semantics, domain and range infer types; they do not reject. In the Touch section, the edge "p109 uses kernel function" makes the reasoner infer that p109 is a Method. With the disjointness axiom the data becomes inconsistent; whether to reject the edge is then a policy of the application. SHACL is a language for validating RDF graphs against a set of conditions: a shapes graph checks a data graph (SHACL, Abstract). Hogan et al. put the contrast in one line: while semantic schemata allow for inferring new graph data, validating schemata allow for validating existing graph data (Sec. 3.1.2, p.14). SHACL validates the conditions you write, on the selected graph, under a declared entailment regime (Sec. 1.5); a missing required value is then a violation. We call this closed-world-style checking (our word; W3C does not use it). The four checks on this page are custom code in the SHACL style, not SHACL shapes.

RDF and property graphs

RDF statements have three parts, so they are called triples, and IRIs can appear in all three positions (RDF 1.1 Primer, Sec. 3.1-3.2). A property graph allows a set of property–value pairs and a label to be associated with both nodes and edges, and property graphs can be translated to/from directed edge-labelled graphs without loss of information (Hogan et al. 2021, Sec. 2.1.3, p.7). An ontology can sit on top of either; the W3C standards for reasoning and validation are written for RDF.

Level 3in detail, for the thesis

What our ML-course ontology needs

For the thesis (a KG + GraphRAG tutor for an ML course). This extends the design in the vault note Ontologies for CS, ML and AI - Graph Data Model.

1. Start from competency questions

Step 1 of Noy and McGuinness: sketch a list of questions that a knowledge base based on the ontology should be able to answer, competency questions (Noy & McGuinness 2001, p.5). They also warn: There is no one correct way to model a domain (p.4). The tree and the classes below are one choice. Ours, for the mini-domain:

    2. Node types (classes)

    No published ontology we found has the teaching relations prerequisite-of, example-of and defines together with an ML vocabulary (vault note, search; "not found" is not proof). So the plan is a thin course schema of our own, with alignment IDs into CSO, AIO and OpenAlex.

    3. Relation types, with domain, range and axioms

    4. Axioms that the data must keep

    • Subclass and type (a curated mapping from the taxonomy): inner tree nodes become classes (subClassOf), leaves become individuals (rdf:type). Our individuals stand for method concepts as taught (SVM = the classification SVM of the course), not for fitted models. This lets the tutor expand "classifiers" to SVM and Perceptron.
    • Transitivity of prerequisite-of: dot product → perceptron → SVM gives dot product → SVM. Transitivity spreads every judgement, so prerequisite edges must be reviewed by the instructor first; the three in the mini-graph are proposals.
    • No cycle (DAG) in prerequisite-of: otherwise no learning order exists. In OWL 2 DL a transitive property cannot also be declared irreflexive, so we check this in code.
    • Disjointness (our design choice): a Concept is never a Learning resource. This is what catches "a passage uses a kernel". Domain and range alone (e.g. Concept → Concept) only type the ends; they cannot catch a wrong edge between two concepts.
    • Sufficient-condition rules: ∃uses.KernelFunction ⊑ KernelMethod (allowed in OWL 2 RL). From "SVM uses kernel" and "kernel is a Kernel function" the reasoner classifies SVM as a kernel method. It does not say that every kernel method uses one.

    5. Closed vocabulary and evidence on every edge

    Every Concept comes from the course's own term list. An extractor may only link to it, or propose a new candidate for a human to review. Every edge carries its evidence: passage id, quote, extractor, confidence and a reviewed flag. The "Try to break it" buttons above show three ways this catches errors.

    6. Annotation guide (short form)

    1. defines: the passage says what X is (a kind + what makes it different). A passage that only uses X is a mention, not a definition.
    2. prerequisite-of A → B: a student cannot follow the definition or first example of B without A. A quote that only links A and B, or course order, is weak evidence: mark the edge as proposed until the instructor confirms it. Keep weaker relations (recommended-before, mathematically-uses) separate and not transitive.
    3. example-of E → X: E is an example resource (a worked problem, a demo) that shows X. A data set is not itself the example: link the example to it with uses-dataset (Iris setosa-vs-rest uses-dataset Iris).
    4. uses M → C: method M needs C in its mechanism. Scope it: "SVM uses a kernel function" holds for the kernelised SVM (the linear SVM is the linear-kernel case). Do not use it for history or authorship.
    5. No quote, no edge. If you cannot point to text, write a candidate, not an edge. And a quote is not enough on its own: record whether the quote states the edge, or only supports our reading of it.
    6. Missing is not false. If no edge says "A prerequisite-of B", the graph does not know. Do not read it as "no".

    Step 1 · the picture

    Taxonomy, ontology, knowledge graph: one glance

    Your notes say: a taxonomy is a hierarchy, an ontology adds relations, a knowledge graph is the ontology applied to data. That is a good start. Press the three buttons and see what each one adds to the same data.

    Why the next idea: "a graph of facts" needs a concrete data model. The standard one is RDF: everything is a triple.
    Step 2 · the data model

    RDF: everything is a triple

    A triple is subject, predicate, object. Subjects and predicates are IRIs (global names). Objects are IRIs, literals (text, numbers) or blank nodes (things without a name, like one offering of a course). Add or remove triples; the graph follows.

    Blue = IRI node, amber = literal, purple dashed = blank node. Click a node to highlight its triples in the table.

    Why the next idea: some of these triples describe single courses, others describe what a "course" or "covers" means. Mixing them makes reasoning impossible to explain. So we split them into layers.
    Step 3 · layers

    T-box, R-box and A-box

    The T-box states things about classes. The R-box states things about properties (roles). The A-box states facts about individuals. In RDF it is all one graph; the layer is a way of reading a triple. Move triples between lanes, then check yourself.

    Why the next idea: the T-box and R-box are not decoration. A program can read them and derive new A-box triples. That program is a reasoner.
    Step 4 · reasoning

    A tiny RDFS + OWL-RL reasoner, with reasons

    Rules in the reasoner: subclass, subproperty, domain, range, inverse, transitive, and a disjointness check. Press the button. Every new triple shows which rule made it and from which premises.

    Why the next idea: the reasoner trusts every triple it gets. An LLM extractor does not always say true things. We need a check that says "this does not fit the schema".
    Step 5 · validation

    SHACL-like shapes check what an LLM extracted

    Three candidate triples, as an extractor might read them from real syllabus sentences. Check each against the shapes and the T-box.

    Open world versus closed world

    OWL assumes the world is open: a missing triple is unknown. SHACL checks the data you have, as if it were closed: a missing triple can be a violation. Pick a course and delete its covers triples.
