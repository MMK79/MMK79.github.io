A layered lesson on the question path

What happens when a student asks

A student in CS 156 asked: "What happens when the points are not linearly separable?" This page follows that question, step by step, through the five arms of our experiment, on the real pilot graph. Switch the arm and watch the context the answer model would receive change.

TL;DR

Eleven steps, one budget cap, five arms

  1. Same start. The question is linked to concept ids (alias table plus bge-m3 search over concept names), once, and H and Hc read the same cached seeds. (G, LightRAG, would use its own retrieval; it is not run here.)
  2. The graph arms walk. From the seeds they follow typed edges (PREREQUISITE_OF, IS_A, USES), at most two hops. The typed arm H follows only the relations the router picks; the collapsed arm Hc follows all three. Nothing else differs.
  3. Edges point to passages. Each edge carries evidence records; their passage ids fetch the passage text. That list is fused with the text retriever's list.
  4. One cap for all. The retrieval arms share one cap of 4,000 tokens for the passage text (counted here with the bge-m3 tokenizer, a proxy for the answer model's); the space used differs by arm. The request carries no relation, hop or path, though the choice of passages itself can still differ.
  5. What is built. Linking, walk, ranking, fusion and packing are computed here on the pilot slice. The router is a stand-in. Answer generation (an Alibaba-hosted LLM), book citations and the citation check are design, not built.
  6. A real finding. In the pilot graph no PREREQUISITE_OF edge has passed review, so with the stand-in router's USES + IS_A mask, H and Hc load the same edges and send the same context on all three questions. A narrower mask (for example USES only, in step 3) separates them; so does the non-confirmatory "also load proposed edges" toggle on Q1.
Level 0in one minute

Five ways to answer at the library desk

For anyone. No technical words.

L · From memory

The librarian answers without opening anything.

R · Similar cards

She pulls the cards whose words and meaning look most like the question.

G · Off-the-shelf map

A ready-made map tool, with its default settings, picks the cards.

H · Labelled arrows

She starts at the idea named in the question and follows only the arrows that fit it, for example "uses" and "is a kind of".

Hc · Same arrows, no labels

The same start and the same map, but she follows every arrow, whatever it says.

The rule

Whatever she does, she may hand over only one folder of the same size, and the reader never learns how each card was found.

Level 1for a student

The story of the question path

  1. Problem: a text retriever finds passages that look like the question. A good answer to "not linearly separable" names two ideas the question does not (soft margin, nonlinear transforms); the course treats them in detail in Lecture 15, although the professor sketches both in his reply. Next idea: start from the concept the question names and walk the graph to related concepts.
  2. Problem: the question is words, the graph is concept ids. Fix: entity linking, once per question, shared by all graph arms.
  3. Problem: walking every edge pulls in everything near the seed. Fix: a typed walk: the router picks which relations fit the question, and the walk stops after two hops.
  4. Problem: the answer model needs text, not edges. Fix: every edge has evidence records pointing to passage ids; the passages are fetched by id.
  5. Problem: a bigger context could win on its own. Fix: one token cap, B = 4,000, for every retrieval arm; passages are added in fused order until the next one does not fit.
  6. Problem: the answer could cite a passage that does not support it. Fix (designed): cite book, chapter, section and page, and check each cited sentence with an entailment checker.

    Product design · not part of the thesis arms

    When the tutor should say "I don't know"

    What this section is. The approved design for the tutor app: how it decides to answer or to say "this is not in the course material". It is not built yet, and it is kept out of the five thesis arms above (see the box at the end of this section). The passages and scores you see are real; every cut-off is a placeholder until we tune it.

    Plain words first. A good student who cannot find the answer in the book says "it's not in the book". A weak one makes up something that sounds right. We want the tutor to be the good student, and we need a way to check it, because a language model is very good at sounding right.

    Why "just tell the model" is not enough

    The easy idea is to add one line to the prompt: "if the passages do not contain the answer, say so". Studies show that models often ignore it.

    ChatGPT, only noise passages: refused24.67% Claude 3.5 Sonnet, no passages: abstained84.1% Claude 3.5 Sonnet, with passages: abstained52%

    First bar: in the RGB benchmark every passage was noise, so the right reply was always "I can't answer"; ChatGPT gave it only about one time in four (exact-match count). Bars 2 and 3: in the Sufficient Context study the same model abstained less once retrieved passages were added: passages make models bolder, even when the passages do not answer the question. These are the authors' numbers on their data, not ours. [chen2023rgb, joren2025]

    So the tutor does not rely on one instruction. It stops at four layers, and when it stops it still helps: it shows the three closest passages.

    The four layers: three checks for each question, one step before launch

    Question
    1. LAYER 1 · before writing

      Retrieval-score gate design, not built

      Look at the search results first. The reranker gives every packed passage a score; take the best one. If even the best passage is a poor match, do not ask the language model to write an answer. The scores already exist from the search, so this layer adds no model call, and it aims at the plainest case: the search found nothing close to the question. A low score does not prove that the answer is nowhere in the course; it only says the search did not find it.

      Real numbers: bge-reranker-v2-m3 scores of this question against the passages the selected arm packs (arm R when L or G is selected), shown on a 0-1 scale (the sigmoid of the raw score). The leakage switch above changes them. The cut-off is ours and not tuned.

    3. LAYER 2 · while writing

      Answer only from the passages design, not built

      The prompt tells the model: use only the passages, and if they do not contain the answer (or part of it), reply with one fixed phrase, NOT_IN_MATERIAL. The app looks for that exact phrase. This layer is cheap, but as the bars above show, models do not always obey it, so it is never the only check.

    5. LAYER 3 · after writing

      Check every sentence, retry once, then stop design, not built

      A small checker model, MiniCheck, reads each sentence of the answer together with the passages it cites and asks: do these passages support this sentence? It reads the whole cited text up to its 2,048-token input limit and never cuts it shorter; a sentence whose cited text is longer is marked "unchecked" and logged, never counted as supported.

      answer sentence + cited passages→MiniCheck: supported?→ yes →keep
      no →write the answer once more→ still not supported →"I could not support an answer from the course material."

      Why only one retry: each retry costs another model call and more waiting, and a model that rewrites its own answer does not reliably fix it. HHEM, a second checker, is logged only and never decides.

    6. ↓
    7. LAYER 4 · before launch

      Tune the cut-offs on human-labelled examples design, not built

      Layers 1 and 3 each need a cut-off. We set them on development examples that a person has labelled (answerable or not, supported or not), never by guessing, and we report the result on separate held-out examples as a risk-coverage curve: coverage = the share of questions the tutor answers; risk = the share of those answers that are wrong. A stricter cut-off answers fewer questions; whether it also makes fewer mistakes is what the curve measures.

      A drawing of the idea, not our data: we have no human labels yet. The dot moves with the placeholder cut-off of layer 1.

    9. WHEN IT STOPS

      Show the three closest passages design, not built

      "I don't know" alone is a dead end. So the tutor says "I could not find this in the enabled course material" and then shows the three passages that came closest (the best-scoring of the passages it retrieved), with their place in the course, so the student can read on.     Kept out of the thesis arms. In the experiment (arms L, R, G, H, Hc) the gate, the retry and the sentence check do not change any answer: a gate would fire differently for each arm and its cut-off would be one more tuned setting. The prompt is the same for all arms, including its one abstain phrase. The harness is planned to log the signals that apply (best reranker score, the abstain phrase, the checker scores; L has no passages to score) for every question and arm, so risk-coverage curves per arm can be drawn afterwards, with no extra model calls. LightRAG's own "Sorry, I'm not able to provide an answer" reply is scored as an abstention.
    A short note · what we found and what we chose

    Query variants: do we rewrite the question?

    Plain words first. A good librarian does not search for your exact sentence. She thinks "this is about margins, maybe soft margin and kernels" and looks on those shelves. Some systems let a model do that thinking: they rewrite the question, write several versions of it, or write a fake answer and search with it.

    What we found

    • It is a real, well-studied step: paraphrases merged together (multi-query, RAG-Fusion), a fake answer used as the search text (HyDE, query2doc), splitting a question into smaller ones, and linking the question's words to graph nodes (GraphRAG, LightRAG, HippoRAG).
    • The gain depends on the retriever. One large study (11 methods, 24 retrievers, 12 datasets) found that expansion tended to help weaker retrievers and hurt stronger ones: on one test set a weak retriever (DPR) gained 10.8 points, while a strong one (E5-large-v2) lost 7.6. Its effect on our R arm is not measured. [weller2023]
    • An LLM rewriter brings its own knowledge, and its own errors, into the search: the fake answer can be wrong, and then the search follows the wrong idea.
    • For graph seeds, how you match matters. HippoRAG 2 found that matching the whole question to graph nodes (what our dense linker does) was no better than finding names first, while matching it to whole facts (triples) gained 12.5% recall@5 on average. [gutierrez2025]

    What we chose

    1. No rewriting in the thesis run. It would be a new factor, it can hurt a strong hybrid retriever like R, and it could mix a model's own knowledge into retrieval. At most, later, one shared cached rewrite for R and H alike as a side check.
    2. Name the linker as part of H. H gets the linker and the router; R gets the raw question. The claim says "a graph walk seeded by an entity linker", and we log how often the linker finds no concept at all.
    3. Log G's keywords and count its extra model call in the cost comparison.

    Level 2academic, with sources

    Why the protocol looks like this

    LiteratureGraphRAG systems build an entity graph from text chunks with an LLM and answer from graph neighbourhoods or community summaries (Edge et al.); LightRAG retrieves over entity and relation keys at two levels (Guo et al.). Our arm G is planned to run LightRAG with its defaults as the off-the-shelf graph baseline; no LightRAG run is shown here. Comparisons of RAG and GraphRAG report that each wins on different question types, so the strata are reported separately. [edge2024, guo2024, han2025]
    LiteratureA passage that supports a claim is not proof that the model used it (Wallat et al.), and ALCE measures citation recall (is each statement supported by its citations?) and citation precision (is each citation needed?) with an entailment model (Gao et al.); our designed check covers only part of that. MiniCheck is a small checker close to GPT-4 on grounding benchmarks (Tang et al.). [wallat2024, gao2023, tang2024]
    Ours, measuredOn 76 claim-premise pairs in 23 clusters (some constructed; labels by one LLM, not human gold), MiniCheck reached balanced accuracy 0.732 (95% interval 0.622-0.852) on the whole premise and 0.454 and 0.501 under two ways of cutting it at 512 tokens. So the designed check scores the whole cited context when it fits MiniCheck's 2,048-token input, logs longer cases instead of cutting them, and never truncates at 512. HHEM is kept as a robustness score only.
    Ours, the protocolOne shared pipeline, link → route → walk → collect → dedupe → rank → fuse(R) → pack(B) → serialize → answer. H and Hc differ only in eligible(edge, route): in H it is "relation in the router's mask", in Hc it is "true". The protocol specifies fourteen rules against the leaks of our own pipeline (the request carries only (opaque id, text) pairs, the Hc graph is a label-stripped projection, neighbours are sorted by id, only asserted edges load, ranking is type-blind, caches are keyed by arm) and ten harness tests that must pass before the main run. This page implements a demo of several rules and passes a small subset of checks (allowlist, shared cached seeds, no label strings in the projected Hc graph, cap never exceeded, leakage, determinism); the full harness is not built.
    Ours, the decision ruleTwo contrasts in a fixed order, each an exact one-sided McNemar test at α = 0.05 on the fully-correct rate of the primary stratum: H − R first; H − Hc is tested confirmatorily only if the first rejects. Both estimates are reported in every case, and the joint claim needs both rejections. A positive H − Hc shows that type-based edge selection beats no selection with this router; it does not show that the labels' meaning matters beyond selectivity.
