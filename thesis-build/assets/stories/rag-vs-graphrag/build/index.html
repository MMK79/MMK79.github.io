Step 1 · the problem

A language model answers fluently. You cannot check it.

Peng et al. name three weak points of a plain LLM. It can hallucinate (invent facts). It lacks domain or private knowledge. Its facts can be out of date. Here we test the first one on a small scale: we ask a model with no sources at all.

hallucinationno domain or proprietary knowledgeoutdated information
Why the next idea: the model does not say "I do not know", and it shows no source. So give it sources: look the facts up first, then answer from them.
Step 2 · the first fix

RAG: retrieve passages, then generate

Retrieval-augmented generation looks up passages that match the question and puts them in the prompt. No retraining is needed. The answer can then cite a passage id. Pick a retriever and change k (how many passages go in).

Why the next step: this works well when one passage holds the answer. Ask a harder question and it breaks.
Step 3 · where RAG struggles

Chains of facts, whole-corpus questions, and noise

Peng et al. list two weak points of RAG: it neglects relations (passages are treated as isolated; similarity cannot express a link), and it carries redundant text (long prompts, "lost in the middle"). We add a third that shows in the data: questions that need the whole corpus.

Why the next idea: the missing piece is a link between two passages. Store that link on purpose, as a graph.
Step 4 · the second fix

GraphRAG: keep the links, then retrieve along them

An LLM reads each passage and writes entities and relations. Identical entities from different passages merge into one node. A node now remembers every passage that mentions it, so passages are connected. Retrieval can follow edges, paths or whole communities.
