Evaluation · a layered lesson

Statistical tests

How do you decide that one retrieval system answers more questions correctly than another, and not by luck? This page explains the tests our thesis uses, four times over: as a picture, for a student, with the research, and as the rules in our thesis. One real example runs through all of it: two ways to cut a lecture into chunks, compared on the same 40 questions (an LLM judge graded whether the retrieved text was enough to answer).

TL;DR

  • In a paired design every system answers the same questions. So we compare the systems question by question, not two separate averages.
  • McNemar's test looks only at the questions where the two systems disagree: b = only the first is right, c = only the second is right. If the systems were equally good (and the questions independent), then, given the number of disagreements, each one would go either way like a fair coin toss.
  • In our example, b = 5 and c = 2. For teaching, we take the direction that favours our rule (chosen after seeing the data; the pilot fixed no test in advance): the exact one-sided tail is 29/128 ≈ 0.23, above 0.05. With 40 questions this is inconclusive, not "no difference": such a small sample has little power for modest effects.
  • For a given test, α and number of questions, power depends on how often the systems disagree (ψ) and on the true difference (δ). For ψ = 0.30 and δ = 0.10 the exact minimum is 269 questions for power 0.90; the thesis target is 270.
  • The thesis claim needs two tests in a fixed order (graph vs text first, then typed vs collapsed labels). The claim holds only if both reject, so neither test needs a smaller α. If the first test does not reject, the second is not tested.
  • "+0.3 SD" (Cohen's d) is a gap between two group averages measured in standard deviations: the average treated student would sit at about the 62nd percentile of the control group. Bloom's 2 SD is the 98th. See Effect size.
  • Bootstrap intervals show how big the difference may be; they are reported but never decide. Cohen's κ measures how far two raters of the same items agree beyond chance.

Two librarians, the same 40 questions

For anyone. No technical words.

Two librarians each get the same 40 questions. For each question, each one brings back a pile of pages, and a referee says whether the pile holds the answer. You want to know: is one librarian really better?

Both succeed, or both fail

33 questions. Here the two librarians got the same result, so these questions do not enter the test.

Only one succeeds

7 questions. Librarian A won 5, librarian B won 2. Only these 7 "duels" can show a difference.

Is 5 to 2 a real lead?

Toss a fair coin 7 times. You get 5 or more heads about 1 time in 4. So a 5-to-2 lead for A can easily happen by luck.

The 40 questions as dots. Grey: both librarians agree. Amber: only A succeeds. Blue: only B succeeds. The test reads the coloured dots only.

Takeaway: the test counts only the duels. A small lead from a few duels can be luck. "We could not see a difference" is not the same as "there is no difference": with only 7 duels, a modest real difference would usually go unseen.
Level 1for a student

Paired data, one table, one coin

For a student who knows what a proportion and a probability are.

Paired designs

In our thesis every arm (system variant) answers every question in the locked set. Each question then gives a pair of outcomes: (arm 1 correct?, arm 2 correct?). The two outcomes on one question are related, so the two accuracy rates are not independent, and a test for two independent proportions is the wrong tool. When the arms tend to succeed and fail on the same questions, the paired comparison is also more precise.

The 2×2 table of the running example

In our chunking pilot, 40 homework questions of Caltech CS 156 were sent to two retrieval rules: our section rule (chunks never cross a 5-minute window of a lecture) and a sentence-packing rule. An LLM judge graded each retrieved text; "correct" here means grade 2, "the text holds what the question needs".

The accuracy of the section rule is (7 + 5)/40 = 30%; of the sentence rule, (7 + 2)/40 = 22.5%. The difference is (b − c)/n = (5 − 2)/40 = 7.5 points. The concordant cells (7 and 26) appear in both rates and cancel in the numerator of the difference; they still count in n, in the estimate and in its uncertainty, but not in McNemar's test.

McNemar's idea

If the two rules are equally good (the null hypothesis H0) and the questions are independent, a disagreement is equally likely to go either way. So, given that there are b + c = 7 disagreements, b behaves like the number of heads in 7 fair coin tosses. The exact one-sided p-value is the chance of 5 or more heads: (21 + 7 + 1)/128 = 29/128 ≈ 0.227. That is above α = 0.05. A caution: the pilot fixed no test in advance, and we chose the direction after seeing the counts, so this number is a teaching example, not a confirmatory test.

What a p-value is, and is not

It isthe probability, computed as if H0 and the test's model were true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. Here: "if the rules were equally good, then, given 7 disagreements, 5 or more of them favour our rule 23% of the time" (one direction; in either direction, 45%).
It is notthe probability that H0 is true; not the size or the importance of the effect; and a p above 0.05 is not evidence that the effect is zero .

Power, ψ and δ

Power is the probability that the test rejects H0 when a real difference exists. For a given test, α and number of questions n (independent questions), McNemar's power depends on two numbers: the discordance rate ψ (the share of questions on which the arms disagree) and the true difference δ. Our pilot observed ψ ≈ 7/40 = 0.175 and a difference of 0.075. If those were the true values, the exact power with 40 questions would be only about 0.17: about 5 of 6 such studies would miss the difference. The pilot does not establish these values; the point is that a sample this small has little power, so a non-rejection here is inconclusive.

Confidence intervals: how big could it be?

A bootstrap resamples the 40 questions with replacement many times, keeps each question's pair together, and recomputes the difference each time. The middle 95% of those differences is a percentile interval. For our example it runs from −5 to +20 points: a wide range, from a small negative to a large positive difference. With only 40 questions, its real coverage can differ from the nominal 95%. The thesis reports such intervals as estimates; it never uses them to decide.

Do two raters agree?

When a label such as "correct" comes from a rater, a second rater on the same items shows how far the labels agree. Cohen's κ compares the observed agreement between the two raters with the agreement that two independent raters with the same label rates would reach. When it is defined, κ = 1 is perfect agreement and 0 is that chance level.

Effect sizewhat "+0.3 SD" means

What "+0.3 SD" means

Papers on tutoring and AI tutors report their gains in "SD". This section explains the unit. Our own thesis claim is a different unit (a difference in the share of questions answered correctly), see Level 3.

The problem: points on one test are not points on another

A study says "the tutored students gained 6 points". On a 20-point quiz that is a lot. On a 200-point exam it is little. To compare studies, we need a unit that does not depend on the test.

The idea: measure the gap in units of the spread

The standard deviation (SD) says how far students typically sit from their group's average. Divide the gap between the two group averages by that spread. The result is the standardised mean difference, also called Cohen's d.

d = (meantreated − meancontrol) / SDpooled

"+0.3 SD" therefore means: the average treated student scores 0.3 standard deviations above the average control student. If scores are roughly bell-shaped with the same spread in both groups, that student sits at about the 62nd percentile of the control group. Move the slider and watch it.

Two bell curves and one slider

control grouptreated groupcontrol students below the average treated student

Both groups are drawn as normal curves with SD = 1; only the treated curve moves. The percentile is exact only under that assumption (bell-shaped scores, equal spread). Real score distributions are rarely exactly so, and a ceiling or floor on the test changes the picture. The Kraft label is meant for causal pre-K-12 studies with standardised tests; the other presets are shown for scale and do not all fit that description.

How to read sizes in education

Cohen's old rule of thumb (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large) came from psychology, not from schools. For education, Kraft looked at causal studies of pre-K-12 achievement on standardised tests and proposed: below 0.05 small, 0.05 to below 0.20 medium, 0.20 or above large . Typical effects in that literature are small in SD terms, so an effect of 0.2 SD already counts as large there. The labels are for that kind of study; they do not carry over to a 3-week lab lesson or a university physics class.

Why Bloom's 2 SD is huge

At 2 SD the average tutored student beats about 98% of the control class. That is ten times Kraft's threshold for a "large" effect (0.20) and about three times the next-largest effect in the table (0.63). Later syntheses find far less for tutoring (0.29 SD pooled over RCTs), and the original rests on a few short studies with narrow tests that bundled mastery learning with tutoring . This is why this thesis states Bloom's number as what he reported, not as what to expect.

Take-away. "+0.3 SD" is a gap measured in units of the students' own spread. Always ask four things: which test, which comparison group, how long, and whether the SD is the pooled spread of one group or of a whole population.

The methods and their sources

With citations. Each citation points to the primary source; a citation we could not check in the primary text is marked.

McNemar's test and its variants

McNemar derived the standard error of the difference between two correlated proportions and showed that the test of that difference depends only on the two discordant cells; its chi-square form is (b − c)²/(b + c) with one degree of freedom . (We could not read McNemar's or Edwards' papers; the formulas are checked in Fagerland et al., who restate them .) Edwards added a continuity correction, (|b − c| − 1)²/(b + c) ; its one-sided upper-tail form is the zcc below. Conditional on b + c, b is Binomial(b + c, 1/2) under H0, which gives the exact conditional test used on this page.

z = (b − c) / √(b + c)     one-sided with correction: zcc = (b − c − 1) / √(b + c)     pexact = P(B ≥ b), B ~ Bin(b + c, ½)     pmid = pexact − ½P(B = b)

Fagerland, Lydersen and Laake compared these versions over many sample sizes and found the exact conditional test conservative: in their study (two-sided tests at a nominal 5%, up to 100 pairs) its actual type I error was "barely above 3%", which costs power ; the continuity-corrected asymptotic test was even more conservative. They recommend the mid-p test or the asymptotic test without correction, and "do not recommend use of the McNemar exact conditional test nor the asymptotic test with CC in any situation" .

Open contradiction with our choiceThe thesis uses the exact conditional test, which this comparison calls conservative. We keep it because, under its model (independent pairs, direction fixed in advance), its type I error is at most α at every n, which matters for a pre-registered decision with a small and uncertain sample. The cost is paid in power, and our power table is computed for the exact test, so the sample-size target already includes that cost. A mid-p analysis could be added as a sensitivity result; it would not decide.

For machine learning, Dietterich compared five tests for "does algorithm A beat algorithm B on this task" and found McNemar's test had low type I error; for algorithms that can be run only once it was the only test with acceptable type I error . The version he studied was the continuity-corrected, two-sided chi-square form, not our exact one-sided form , and it does not measure variation due to the training set. Our setting is similar: each arm answers each locked question once (the label is a majority of three generations), and the test applies to those fixed paired labels if the questions are independent.

One-sided tests and the p-value

The thesis hypotheses are directional (the graph arm is better), so the tests are one-sided at α = 0.05, and this was fixed before any main result. A one-sided test cannot support the opposite direction; a result in that direction is reported as an estimate. The American Statistical Association's statement lists what a p-value does not tell you: it is not the probability that the hypothesis is true or that the data arose by chance alone, it does not measure the size or importance of an effect, and a scientific conclusion should not rest only on whether p passes a threshold .

Power and sample size

For paired binary outcomes, the power of McNemar's test depends on the difference Δ = p10 − p01 and the discordance ψ = p10 + p01; note |Δ| ≤ ψ. At fixed Δ, a higher ψ means more noise from disagreements and needs more questions. Because the decision uses the exact test, the thesis computes power exactly: the number of disagreements is Binomial(n, ψ), and the conditional rejection probability is averaged over all of them. Two hypotheses: fixed sequence and intersection-union

The thesis makes two standalone statements (H1: H beats R; H2: H beats Hc) and one joint claim (both). Testing H1 first and H2 only if H1 rejects, each at the full α, keeps the family-wise error rate at most α for the two statements; it needs no α adjustment, and after the first non-rejection no later hypothesis can be declared significant . The reason: if H1's null is true, any false rejection must start with a false rejection of H1 (probability at most α); if H1's null is false, only H2 can be falsely rejected (probability at most α). For the joint claim, the null hypothesis is the union "H1's null or H2's null is true". Berger showed that rejecting a union of nulls when every component test rejects at level α gives a level-α test, with no adjustment . Regulatory guidance states the same for co-primary endpoints: when success needs every endpoint, "there is no multiplicity problem", but power falls . Requiring both tests is the price: the joint power is no greater than the smaller of the two powers. Since P(A ∩ B) ≥ P(A) + P(B) − 1 for any two events, power 0.90 for each gives at least 0.80 for both.

Holm's procedure: when you would need it

If several hypotheses are tested with no fixed order and any rejection counts as a finding, the family-wise error must be controlled across them. Holm's step-down procedure sorts the k p-values and compares the i-th smallest with α/(k − i + 1), stopping at the first non-rejection; it controls the family-wise error rate and rejects at least as much as Bonferroni . The thesis does not need it for H1 and H2 (their order is fixed), and it reports strata and exploratory arms as estimates only, outside the confirmatory family. If, for example, the thesis tested H1 separately in each stratum and claimed every stratum that rejected, it would need a multiplicity procedure such as Holm's. Intervals for strata are individual, not simultaneous, so they must not be read as extra confirmatory findings.

Bootstrap confidence intervals

The bootstrap estimates the sampling distribution of a statistic by resampling the observed units with replacement; the percentile interval takes the 2.5% and 97.5% points of the resampled statistic . For paired data, a resample draws whole questions (all arm outcomes together). In general it should resample the independent sampling units: in the thesis these are source clusters (one homework stem, one book exercise). The interval answers "how big might the difference be"; the test answers "should we reject H0". They can disagree at small counts: with b = 4, c = 0 and 40 questions, the percentile interval from resampling questions (with infinitely many resamples) is [2.5, 20] points, which excludes 0, while the exact p = 0.0625 does not reject. The nominal 95% does not guarantee 95% coverage at such sizes. A pre-registered analysis must say in advance which one decides.

Agreement: Cohen's κ

Cohen defined κ = (po − pe)/(1 − pe), where po is the observed share of agreement and pe the agreement expected by chance from the two raters' marginal rates . Because pe depends on the marginals, the same raw agreement gives very different κ values when one label is rare (calculator 4). So κ is reported together with the raw agreement. A reference rater is not the truth: two raters can share errors.

The rules our thesis fixed in advance

What the protocol says, word for word where it matters. These are planning rules, not results.

The two testsOn the locked question set, H1: the typed hybrid H has a higher fully-correct rate than the text retriever R; H2: H has a higher rate than H-collapsed (the same retrieval code on the same graph with the three relation labels collapsed to one). Every test is an exact one-sided McNemar test on (b, c) at α = 0.05, for every value of b + c (no chi-square switch). Each report gives b, c, the p-value and the sidedness.
The questions30 to 60 questions, extracted from the 7 reference books.
The decisionH1 is tested first; H2 only if H1 is rejected. The claim holds if and only if both are rejected. If H1 is not rejected, H2 is not tested. If H1 is rejected and H2 is not, the thesis says: "the graph helps under this cap; the labels add no detectable gain" (no evidence of an extra gain, which is not evidence of none). δ = 0.10 is a planning value for the sample size, not a minimum important effect.
The sample sizePlanning value ψ = 0.30 for both contrasts. The exact minimum for power 0.90 is 269 (power 0.9000); the target is 270 (power 0.9010), which gives a joint power of at least 0.802. For power 0.80 the minimum is 199. The pilot can only raise the target (if the one-sided 80% upper Clopper-Pearson bound of the pilot ψ is above 0.30), never lower it. A second model family recomputed these numbers and corrected the earlier table, whose values were not exact minima (for ψ = 0.30: 202 → 199 and 270 → 269); 270 stays as the target.
Inconclusive, not no-effectThe question set holds 30 to 60 questions, far below the target. The status line therefore says in advance: "below that target, a non-rejection is reported as inconclusive and not as evidence of no effect. A null or negative result is a finding." At power 0.80 and ψ between 0.20 and 0.30, independent items assumed, the exact test with n = 60 detects a difference of Δ ≈ 0.150 to 0.182; with n = 30 and ψ = 0.20, no difference up to ψ reaches power 0.80 (at ψ = 0.30 it needs Δ ≈ 0.256). Only large differences are detectable; the thesis reports this sentence for the achieved n.
Intervals and independenceA paired cluster bootstrap (10,000 resamples, fixed seed) gives two-sided 95% percentile intervals; they never decide. The primary stratum keeps at most one item per source cluster (one homework stem, one book exercise). The test and the power numbers assume independent items; the cluster rule reduces dependence but cannot prove independence.
The manipulation check (gate 3), a precondition for H2H2 compares H with H-collapsed. If the two arms send the same request to the answer model, the contrast says nothing about the labels. So s is the share of the 30 pilot-2 items on which the requests of H and H-collapsed differ; because |E(pH − pHc)| ≤ s, if the exact one-sided 95% upper bound of s is below 0.10 (that is, s = 0/30; 1/30 gives 0.149), or if no primary item differs, the mechanism clause is removed from the claim before the main run, and H2 is reported as an estimate with the bound s. The gate is evaluated once and its decision is final. As first registered, pilot 1 measured s on its 20 primary items: 0/20, bound 0.139, not triggered; a 0/20 could never trigger, which is why the stricter rule replaced it.
The pilot power check (gate 4)The pilot can only raise the target. On the 20 primary items of pilot 1, ψ̂(H, R) = 3/20 with a one-sided 80% upper bound of 0.259, and ψ̂(H, Hc) = 2/20 with an upper bound of 0.202. Both are at most 0.30, so the target stays 270.
What is not testedStrata, the other arms (closed book, LightRAG, exploratory variants), faithfulness and recall are reported as estimates with intervals and no p-values. They make no confirmatory claims, so the family stays at two tests and no multiplicity procedure such as Holm's is needed. One exception is fixed in advance: a secondary proxy (fully correct and citing at least one gold passage) gets the exact one-sided McNemar test on H − R and H − Hc in every case, labelled "secondary, unadjusted"; it never enters the decision, and one decorative gold citation passes it, so it is not a measure of grounding.

The running example shows why these rules exist. Our chunking pilot had 40 questions and an observed ψ ≈ 0.175; its best-looking contrast (5 vs 2, direction chosen after seeing the data) gave a tail of about 0.23 and an interval from −5 to +20 points. With 40 questions, ψ = 0.30 and a true gain of 10 points, the power would be only about 0.22: such a run usually can neither establish nor exclude a 10-point gain. Writing the decision rule, the sample-size target and the "inconclusive" wording before the main run is what keeps such a result honest.
